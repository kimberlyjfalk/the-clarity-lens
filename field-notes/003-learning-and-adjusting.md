THE CLARITY LENS | PART I: Understanding the Present

FIELD NOTE 003

I've been thinking about why organizations sometimes resist new information because responding to it feels like changing the strategy.
We like decisions.

Once we've debated something, agreed on a direction and started executing, there's a natural desire to keep moving.

Then reality gives us something new.

After the leadership offsite I wrote about in Field Note 001, we had a direction and each function was executing against it.

I tested an industry where we had relatively few customers. We spent very little. The response was interesting. I went back to our customer data.

The customers we had were reasonably strong. I dug deeper.

Why had they bought? What had they bought? How long had they stayed? What problems were they solving? 

Should this be a primary target market? Maybe. Maybe not. That's not the point.

We had learned something we didn't know when we made the original decisions.

And that didn't necessarily require changing the strategy. It required understanding what the information meant.

What if Product, Sales and Marketing had stopped and together, compare it to the opportunities we were already pursuing?

Maybe nothing should change.

Maybe Marketing should test further. Maybe Sales should look for similar accounts. Maybe Product should understand what these customers valued.

And maybe, eventually, we'd learn something significant enough to influence the broader strategy.

But none of that required a pivot. It required deeper learning and understanding.

I think organizations often blur those two things.

Once a strategy has been agreed upon, adjusting what we're doing can feel like questioning the decision we've already made.

So new information gets discussed quickly. Someone points to what has historically been true. Everyone is busy. No one wants to seemingly pivot yet again.

We keep moving, because movement feels like progress.

But a strategy shouldn't prevent us from responding to what we're learning.

It should give us the context to understand what that learning means and the flexibility to adjust.

That's one of the reasons shared understanding matters.

When people understand why you're pursuing something, what assumptions it's based on and how the pieces fit together, new information doesn't have to create organizational whiplash.

You have something against which to evaluate it.

Does this reinforce what we understand? Challenge an assumption? Suggest we should refine something? Is it significant enough to investigate further?

And only then: does it tell us something important enough that the strategy itself should change?

Most new information won't. Some may improve execution. Some deepen what we understand. Some won't matter. And occasionally, something will change how we see the business.

The point isn't to continually change direction. It's to continually learn.

The present doesn't stand still.

Shared understanding shouldn't either.
