# The Clarity Lens | Part I: Understanding the Present

## Field Note 001

I've been thinking about why organizations frequently strategize before they've developed a shared understanding of the present.

Most leadership teams would probably say they already have one.
They know their customers, ICP, market data, what's working and what isn't.

And they've spent time together discussing it. I remember one leadership offsite where we did exactly that.

We sliced and diced customer segmentation, ARR, top industries, and so on. We used it to help define our ICP.

We looked at the same data, discussed it and agreed on a direction.

But afterward, we hadn't necessarily developed the same understanding of what the data meant.

In marketing I saw several industries worth understanding more deeply. Spend and longevity made some even more compelling.

Sales saw something different. One industry had a high number of prospects in the database, so Sales worked that audience.

Product was focused elsewhere. Quickly built capabilities still needed work and remained important to the product direction.

And over all of this sat the revenue target. We had a number to hit.

That pressure has a way of simplifying strategy very quickly.

Marketing needs to drive pipeline. Sales needs to sell more. Product needs to support future growth.

All reasonable.

But without shared understanding underneath those decisions, the revenue goal can start functioning as the strategy itself.

Everyone is trying to grow revenue, but from different understandings of where growth should come from.

And then something else happens.

When revenue becomes the strategy, strategy starts changing every time the path to the number changes.

A quarter is behind. A large opportunity appears. Pipeline is short. A customer asks for something important. Priorities follow.

Over time, strategy can become a series of responses to whatever appears most likely to help hit the number.

Sales saw volume. Marketing saw value and longevity. Product saw an existing commitment that needed to be completed.

None was inherently irrational.

But were we collectively making the best decisions for the company?

Shared information isn't shared understanding.

Neither is agreement.

And neither is a shared revenue goal.

The work before strategy isn't simply gathering data and agreeing on a direction.

It's developing a shared and realistic understanding of the present: our business, our customers, our market and the opportunities in front of us.

It's understanding what the data is telling us and why, then bringing those perspectives together to make stronger collective choices about how and where to focus now and what we're building toward.

That gives us the best opportunity to grow. Not just hit a number.

You cannot intentionally build the future until you have a shared understanding of the present.
