THE CLARITY LENS | PART I: Understanding the Present

FIELD NOTE 003

I've been thinking about what a true shared understanding of an organization actually looks like.

Most organizations can consistently tell you who they are. They have a mission. A vision. Values. An ICP. Revenue targets. A strategy.

But I'm increasingly convinced that's not the same as having a shared understanding of the business.

Knowing who you are is different from understanding how the organization actually exists today.

How mature is each part of the business? Where are we genuinely strong? What can we support today, not theoretically, but well? Where are decisions in one area creating consequences somewhere else? What assumptions are we operating from, and which have we actually tested?

These questions are harder to put on a strategy slide. They're also harder to answer because the answers may challenge what we're already doing.

Product may be capable of building faster than the rest of the organization can absorb. Sales may pursue customers Marketing doesn't believe represent the best growth opportunity. Marketing may create demand the organization isn't ready to support. A company may want to move upmarket before its product, sales motion, or customer experience is ready to move with it.

None of that necessarily means something is broken.

It means understanding one part of the organization isn't enough. You have to understand it in relation to the others.

We tend to describe organizations by their parts--Product. Sales. Marketing. Customer Success. Finance. People.

But customers don't experience those parts independently. Neither does growth. A decision in one place changes the conditions somewhere else.

So perhaps a true shared understanding is different perspectives coming together to dig into how the organization is actually functioning today: its purpose, capabilities, constraints, maturity, relationships, assumptions and readiness for what's next.

With a goal to understand the whole.

And that's where the real value becomes apparent.

The purpose of shared understanding isn't to create more agreement. It's to enable greater autonomy without losing coherence.

When teams are making decisions from shared context about the business, its opportunities, what it can support and where it's trying to go, they can move independently without moving in different directions. 

Priorities and tradeoffs become clearer. New information has context.

That's why I think organizations sometimes have the relationship between clarity and speed backwards.

Developing shared understanding can feel like slowing down. But the time spent establishing it isn't the opposite of speed.

It's what eventually makes speed possible.

Maybe that's the difference between alignment and shared understanding.

Alignment asks: Do we agree on where we're going?

Shared understanding asks: Are we starting from the same understanding of where and who we are?
