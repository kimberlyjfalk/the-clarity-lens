Introduction: The Clarity Lens

You cannot intentionally build the future until you have a shared understanding of the present.

For most of my career, I've found myself asking questions that sometimes seemed to slow the conversation down, particularly at the leadership level.

What problem are we actually trying to solve? Do we agree on what our customers really need? How does this decision affect the rest of the business? Are we solving the same problem?

Often, the response was some version of: We know all this. Let's just get moving.

Organizations are under constant pressure to move. Launch the product. Exceed the numbers. Enter the market. Build the strategy. Fix the pipeline.

Movement feels like progress.

But I've too often watched, and had to compensate for, what happens when organizations move quickly without first developing a true, shared understanding of who and where they really are.

Teams make individually reasonable decisions that don't work together. Strategies get created from different assumptions. Sales and Marketing appear misaligned. Products get launched before the rest of the organization knows how to sell, position or support them.

Eventually, the organization finds itself fixing problems that aren't really the problem.

Over time, I've come to believe something very simple:

You cannot intentionally build the future until you have a shared understanding of the present.

And by "the present," I don't just mean knowing your mission, ICP, firmographics or what's written in the strategy deck.

I mean really understanding the organization as it actually exists today.

Who are we? Where do we play? What do our customers actually value? Where are we strong? Where are we not? What assumptions are we operating from?

And do the people making decisions across the organization share that understanding?

Most organizations believe they know the answers to these questions. Often, I’ve found the answers aren’t genuinely shared or they simply don’t know.

I've also come to believe that clarity isn't something an organization achieves once.

Organizations grow. Markets change. Customers change. Capabilities evolve. Decisions made in one part of the business affect another.

Understanding has to evolve too.

That's what I've started calling The Clarity Lens.

It's not a framework. It's a body of observations I’ve collected over years of helping organizations position themselves, grow, and evolve.
