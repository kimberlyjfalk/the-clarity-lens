THE CLARITY LENS | PART I: Understanding the Present

FIELD NOTE 005

I've been thinking about why so many organizations describe who they are by describing what they sell.

It happens all the time.

Often a company website is a description of its product(s): platform, capabilities, technology, and features.

Often the company description is just a broader version of product.

Features become benefits: Faster. Easier. More secure. More intelligent.

But I still don’t understand the company.

What challenges does solve? Why does that matter? Where does it create distinctive value? Why this solution?

Why does this happen so often?

I think part of the answer is that products are concrete. They're relatively easy to describe.

Understanding the role the company plays for its customers is harder. Not because organizations don't know their customers. They usually know a great deal about them.

The harder questions are: What customer challenges are we uniquely positioned to solve? Where does the value we create matter most?

That requires looking beyond the product itself and understanding the relationship between what customers need, what we do particularly well, where the market is going, what competitors can credibly claim and what we want the organization to become.

And it requires choices. The answer isn't everything the company sells or everything its products can do.

That's where organizations get uncomfortable. 

Choosing can feel like leaving value on the table: What about this use case, the feature we just built, our next market entry?

So, we add them.

The company story gets broader. The product story gets longer. The language gets more abstract. 

Eventually, we've created something everyone inside the company can agree is accurate, but very few people outside the company immediately understand, or see as differentiated.

I've seen this happen even when a company and its products are genuinely differentiated.

And that's why I think company positioning and product positioning are often treated as messaging exercises when they're really understanding exercises.

They're related, but they're not the same.

The company story should help me understand the challenges you exist to solve and the value you uniquely provide.

The product story should help me understand how a particular product delivers against that promise.

Marketing can write clearer words and simplify the website, but it can't manufacture clarity the organization itself doesn't have.

The clearer the organization becomes about owning the role it plays and where its value matters most, the less work the language has to do. Greatly reducing the explaining that waters sales decks and slows the sales cycle. 

You don't have to make the company or its products sound extraordinary. Just make their value recognizable.

That's why the strongest positioning often feels obvious once you hear it.

Not because it was easy to find. Because the work of clarity happened first.
